**The argument.** When people use something wrongly, the design is at fault, not the person. Norman builds his case on doors that must be pushed but look like they should be pulled, stoves whose knobs do not match the burners, and switches nobody can label from memory. From these small failures he draws a vocabulary that applies to any interface, including software: good design makes it plain what can be done, shows what is happening, and forgives mistakes.

## Ideas worth keeping

- **Discoverability.** A person should be able to tell what actions are possible and how to do them, without a manual.
- **Signifiers and affordances.** A label, an icon or a shape tells you where to act. The object itself decides what is possible.
- **Feedback.** Every action should visibly result in something, quickly. Silence feels like failure.
- **Conceptual model.** People form a picture of how a thing works. The design should make that picture correct.
- **Constraints and forcing functions.** Make the wrong action hard, and make the dangerous step deliberate.
- **Human error is design feedback.** Slips and mistakes are different, and each needs its own prevention, detection and recovery.

## Where it shows in Classroom Manager

A teacher uses the program in front of a class, with no time to read and every error in public. So controls carry clear labels and icons, the help guide shows real screenshots, and risky actions such as Exam mode ask for confirmation. A display change that nobody confirms goes back by itself after 15 seconds. Anyone who builds, buys or chooses tools for people who are not technical, including teachers and school IT staff who evaluate software.
